1. WHAT IS THE DETECTOR AND WHAT ARE WE TRYING TO UNDERSTAND?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We are building a Bulk Micromegas particle detector called the "P2 basket".
The core part of this detector is a very thin stainless steel wire mesh
(like a fine metallic fabric) that is stretched flat over a printed circuit
board (PCB). The mesh must stay perfectly flat and taut to work correctly —
any waves or wrinkles in the mesh ruin the detector performance.

One step in the manufacturing process is BAKING: we heat the detector to
146 degrees Celsius for a period of time. This step is necessary for the
detector to function correctly (it cures the adhesive that holds things in
place).

THE PROBLEM: After baking, we sometimes see that the mesh has developed small
waves or wrinkles. This is called "buckling". We want to understand:

  (a) WHY does the mesh buckle during baking?
  (b) WHEN exactly does it happen (at what temperature, at what mesh tension)?
  (c) How can we PREVENT it from happening?


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHAT HOLDS THE MESH IN PLACE? (THE STRUCTURE)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The mesh is not just floating — it is bonded to the PCB at many points:

  - WALL FRAME: The edges of the mesh are glued to the outer frame of the PCB
    all around the perimeter. This is a 1 mm wide border.

  - PILLARS: Inside the detector, there are small columns of adhesive
    (called "Dynamask photoresist pillars") that stick up from the PCB and
    are bonded to the underside of the mesh. These pillars are arranged in a
    regular grid with 3 mm spacing between them. They hold the mesh at a
    controlled distance from the PCB surface.

The mesh is stretched (pre-tensioned) before being bonded. This tension is
what keeps it flat — like the skin of a drum.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. WHY DOES BAKING CAUSE BUCKLING? (THE PHYSICAL MECHANISM)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All materials expand when heated. The key issue here is that the stainless
steel mesh and the PCB DO NOT EXPAND BY THE SAME AMOUNT.

When you heat something by 1 degree Celsius:
  - Stainless steel (SS316L) expands by 16 millionths of its length per degree
  - FR4 / PCB material expands by approximately 14 millionths of its length
    per degree (this number is UNCERTAIN — see Section 5)

This difference in expansion rate is small (only 2 millionths per degree) but
over 121 degrees of baking (from 25°C to 146°C) it adds up.

Here is what happens step by step:

  Step 1 — Before baking:
    The mesh is stretched flat. It is under tension (like a drum skin pulled
    tight). This tension is what keeps it flat.

  Step 2 — Heating begins:
    Both the mesh and the PCB start to expand. BUT the PCB expands slightly
    LESS than the mesh would like to.

  Step 3 — The PCB drags the mesh:
    Because the mesh is BONDED to the PCB at the pillars and the wall frame,
    the PCB pulls the mesh inward. The PCB is contracting relative to the
    mesh's natural size. This creates a COMPRESSIVE force inside the mesh
    (a force that pushes inward from all sides, trying to squeeze the mesh).

  Step 4 — Two forces compete:
    - PRE-TENSION: pulls the mesh outward (keeps it flat)
    - BAKING COMPRESSION: pushes the mesh inward (tries to crumple it)

  Step 5 — Buckling:
    If the baking compression grows larger than the pre-tension, the mesh
    loses its ability to stay flat and collapses into waves. This is called
    buckling.

The temperature at which buckling starts depends on how strong the pre-tension
is. Higher tension = can resist more compression = can survive higher
temperatures.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE SIMPLE FORMULA (HOW WE CALCULATE THIS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We describe the competition between pre-tension and baking compression using
a single number called LAMBDA (λ):

    λ  =  Pre-tension force  /  Baking compression force

    λ > 1  →  pre-tension wins  →  mesh survives baking flat
    λ = 1  →  balanced exactly  →  mesh buckles at exactly 146°C
    λ < 1  →  compression wins  →  mesh buckles BEFORE end of baking

If λ < 1, we can also calculate at what temperature buckling first appears:

    Temperature at buckling  ≈  25°C  +  λ × 121°C

Example: if λ = 0.5, buckling starts at 25 + 0.5 × 121 = 85°C.

The baking compression force (per unit width of mesh) is:

    Compression  =  (Mesh stiffness)  ×  (CTE difference)  ×  (Temperature rise)

Where:
  - Mesh stiffness   = how strongly the mesh resists being squeezed
                       (depends on wire material, wire size, and weave geometry)
  - CTE difference   = how much more the SS expands than the PCB per degree
                       (CTE = Coefficient of Thermal Expansion)
  - Temperature rise = 146°C − 25°C = 121°C

5. WHAT WE KNOW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

5.1  THE MESH
----------
  Material:       Stainless steel SS316L
  Wire diameter:  18 micrometres (0.018 mm)
  Wire pitch:     45 micrometres (0.045 mm) — centre-to-centre spacing
  CTE of SS316L:  16 × 10⁻⁶ per degree Celsius
                  (this means: 16 millionths of its length per °C)
                  THIS VALUE IS WELL KNOWN AND RELIABLE.

5.2  THE BAKING PROCESS
-------------------
  Starting temperature:  25°C  (room temperature)
  Baking temperature:    146°C
  Temperature rise:      121°C

5.3  THE MESH PRE-TENSIONS WE HAVE TESTED
--------------------------------------
  We have tested four different mesh tensions:
    300 N/m  (= 3 N/cm)
    500 N/m  (= 5 N/cm)
    700 N/m  (= 7 N/cm)
   1000 N/m  (= 10 N/cm)

  (N/m = Newtons per metre, a standard unit for force per unit width.
   Think of it as: how hard you are pulling the mesh per centimetre of width.)

5.4  THE PILLAR STRUCTURE
---------------------
  Pillar material:  Dynamask photoresist (a UV-cured adhesive)
  Pillar pitch:     3 mm (distance between adjacent pillars)
  Wall frame:       1 mm wide glued border around the perimeter

5.5  WHAT WE HAVE OBSERVED EXPERIMENTALLY
--------------------------------------
  - At 7 N/cm: very narrow waves appear after baking
  - At tensions below approximately 10 N/cm: waves appear after baking
  - At tensions at or above 10 N/cm: no visible waves after baking
  - At LOW tensions: waves start appearing during baking at temperatures
    as low as 60°C (not just at the end of the bake)
  - The waves are "narrow" — this suggests they appear between the pillars
    (the pillar spacing of 3 mm sets the scale of the wrinkles)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. WHAT WE DO NOT KNOW (THE MISSING INFORMATION)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

There are two critical unknowns that prevent us from making a reliable
prediction. These are the things we need help finding.


6.1  THE CTE OF THE PCB MATERIAL  [MOST IMPORTANT — HIGH PRIORITY]
--------------------------------------------------------------
  The PCB is made from Shengyi S1000-2M, which is a special high-performance
  FR4-type material. Its exact thermal expansion coefficient has NOT been
  published by the manufacturer.

  Why this matters so much:
    The DIFFERENCE between the CTE of the SS mesh and the CTE of the PCB is
    what drives the buckling. Even a small change in the PCB CTE makes a big
    difference to the result.

    Example:
      If PCB CTE = 14 × 10⁻⁶ /°C  →  difference = 2 × 10⁻⁶ /°C
      If PCB CTE = 12 × 10⁻⁶ /°C  →  difference = 4 × 10⁻⁶ /°C  (DOUBLES the compression!)
      If PCB CTE = 10 × 10⁻⁶ /°C  →  difference = 6 × 10⁻⁶ /°C  (TRIPLES the compression!)

  What we need:
    The in-plane CTE of Shengyi S1000-2M, ideally in the X-Y direction
    (the direction parallel to the PCB surface — NOT the Z direction through
    the thickness, which is a different number and less relevant here).

  How to get it:
    Option A — Ask Shengyi directly. They may have a datasheet value they
               have not published, or they can measure it.
    Option B — Find a colleague who has measured CTE of similar PCB materials
               and can provide a reference or measurement.
    Option C — Measure it: heat a PCB sample of known length and measure
               how much it expands (dilatometry or strain gauge measurement).

  Standard FR4 in-plane CTE is typically between 14 and 18 × 10⁻⁶ /°C.
  High-performance laminates like S1000-2M often have LOWER in-plane CTE
  (sometimes as low as 10–12 × 10⁻⁶ /°C), which would make buckling WORSE
  because the difference with SS increases.


6.2  THE EFFECTIVE STIFFNESS OF THE WOVEN SS MESH
---------------------------------------------
  The mesh is a woven fabric of SS wires. Its resistance to being compressed
  in its own plane is not simply the stiffness of solid stainless steel —
  it depends on the wire weave geometry, wire diameter, and wire pitch.

  For a woven mesh, the effective stiffness can range quite widely depending
  on how tight the weave is and how much the wires are pre-tensioned (which
  can straighten out the natural crimping of the wires).

  Why this matters:
    The compression force the mesh experiences during baking depends directly
    on how stiff the mesh is. A stiffer mesh means more compression force for
    the same thermal mismatch.

  What we need:
    The effective in-plane membrane stiffness of the mesh, expressed as
    E × t  (Young's modulus × effective thickness, in N/m or MN/m).

  How to get it:
    The most reliable method for a pre-tensioned wire mesh is the
    VIBRATION METHOD:
      - Stretch the mesh to a known tension T (in N/m)
      - Measure the resonant (natural) frequency f of the mesh vibrating
        like a drum skin
      - The stiffness can be extracted from the formula:
          f = (1 / 2L) × sqrt(T / ρ_surface)
        where L is the side length and ρ_surface is the mesh mass per unit area.
    This is a well-established technique in Micromegas detector construction.
    Some detector groups (CERN, CEA Saclay) have done this routinely.

  Alternative:
    If someone has already characterised the stiffness of this exact mesh
    (45 μm pitch, 18 μm wire, SS316L) under similar pre-tension, that
    value can be used directly.


6.3  BEHAVIOUR OF THE DYNAMASK ADHESIVE DURING BAKING  [SECONDARY]
-------------------------------------------------------------
  The pillars that hold the mesh are made of Dynamask photoresist. This
  is a UV-cured adhesive. We do not know how its mechanical properties
  change at baking temperatures (does it soften? does it partially release
  the bond?).

  If Dynamask softens significantly at 60–146°C, the pillar bonds could
  partially release, which would reduce the effective pre-tension during
  the hottest part of the bake — making buckling worse.

  What we need:
    The glass transition temperature (Tg) of cured Dynamask photoresist.
    If Tg > 146°C, softening is not an issue.
    If Tg < 146°C, the adhesive softens during baking and this is a
    contributing factor.

  How to get it:
    Contact the Dynamask manufacturer (MRC / Riston / DuPont depending on
    which product is used) and ask for the Tg of their cured film.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. WHAT THE EXPERIMENT ALREADY TELLS US (EVEN WITHOUT THE MISSING DATA)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Even without knowing the individual values of PCB CTE and mesh stiffness,
the experimental observations constrain their COMBINED effect:

  From the observation that 10 N/cm (1000 N/m) is approximately the
  threshold tension for buckling at 146°C, we can calculate the combined
  compression force at full bake:

    Compression at 146°C  ≈  1000 N/m  (≈ 10 N/cm)

  And for lower tensions where waves appear at 60°C (ΔT = 35°C from room):
    Compression at 60°C  ≈  T_buckle × (35/121)

  This tells us the combined product (mesh stiffness × CTE difference):
    Combined factor  ≈  1000 / 121  ≈  8.3  N/m per degree Celsius

  Once we know the PCB CTE (and therefore the CTE difference), we can
  calculate what the mesh stiffness must be, and vice versa.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. HOW TO PREVENT BUCKLING — OPTIONS TO CONSIDER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Based on the physics, there are four ways to prevent the mesh from buckling:

  OPTION 1 — INCREASE PRE-TENSION
    Use a higher mesh tension (above the buckling threshold).
    From experiment, this threshold is around 10 N/cm at 146°C.
    Going to 12–15 N/cm would give a safety margin.
    Constraint: higher tension is mechanically harder to achieve and
    maintain during the gluing process, and may affect detector performance.

  OPTION 2 — REDUCE BAKING TEMPERATURE
    If the process allows, bake at a lower temperature (e.g. 120°C instead
    of 146°C). This reduces the temperature rise ΔT and therefore the
    compression force. The required tension to survive is proportionally lower.
    Constraint: the baking temperature may be required for the Dynamask
    cure or other detector specifications.

  OPTION 3 — CHOOSE A PCB MATERIAL WITH HIGHER CTE
    If the PCB material expanded at the SAME rate as SS (CTE = 16 × 10⁻⁶),
    there would be zero differential expansion and zero buckling compression.
    Using a PCB laminate with CTE closer to 16 × 10⁻⁶ (rather than the
    current ~14 × 10⁻⁶ or lower) would reduce or eliminate the problem.
    Constraint: other PCB properties (dielectric constant, dimensional
    stability, radiation tolerance) may be the primary selection criteria.

  OPTION 4 — REDUCE PILLAR SPACING
    The waves we observe are narrow, approximately matching the 3 mm pillar
    pitch. Reducing pillar spacing (e.g. to 2 mm or 1.5 mm) would increase
    the local buckling resistance of each inter-pillar cell.
    Constraint: detector physics requirements may limit pillar density
    (pillars block or disturb the electric field in the gas gap).

10. WHAT WE HAVE TRIED SO FAR (SIMULATION WORK)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We have set up a finite element model of the mesh using FreeCAD and CalculiX.
The mesh geometry has approximately 100,000 nodes.

We ran two types of simulations:

  GLUE STIFFNESS SWEEP (completed):
    We varied the stiffness of the pillar bond (from very soft to very rigid)
    and looked at how this changes the buckling behaviour. Results showed
    that when pillar stiffness is too high, artificial numerical modes appear
    in the simulation and make the results unreliable. Physically realistic
    stiffness values give cleaner results.

  BAKING BUCKLING SIMULATION (attempted, then replaced by analytical formula):
    We tried to simulate the exact baking scenario in CalculiX. We discovered
    that the version of CalculiX included with FreeCAD 0.20 does NOT support
    thermal loads as the reference load in a buckling analysis step. We
    therefore calculated the buckling threshold using a simple analytical
    formula instead.

  ANALYTICAL RESULT (current best estimate):
    The formula for the buckling safety factor is:

        λ  =  T_pre  /  (K_mesh × Δα × ΔT)

    Where:
        T_pre   = mesh pre-tension [N/m]
        K_mesh  = effective mesh stiffness [N/m] — UNKNOWN, see Section 6.2
        Δα      = CTE difference between SS and PCB [per °C] — UNCERTAIN,
                  see Section 6.1
        ΔT      = 121°C (baking temperature rise)

    This formula is physically exact for a uniform pre-tensioned membrane.
    Its accuracy depends entirely on knowing Δα and K_mesh correctly.
    Right now we are using estimated values for both, which is why the
    predicted threshold does not precisely match the experimental one.
