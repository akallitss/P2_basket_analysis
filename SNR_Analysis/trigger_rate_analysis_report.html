VMM Trigger Rate Analysis
P2 Basket Detector — SPS Beam Test

CERN SPS Beam Test  ·  November 2025 Author: Alexandra Kallitsopoulou Generated: June 2026

Overview

This report documents the five-step trigger rate analysis pipeline applied to VMM3a ASIC configuration scans at two beam intensities: 5 kHz and 15 kHz. Each step is illustrated with plots from both datasets, using VMM 11 (P2 Small Detector 1, connector 1, ch 14–63) and VMM 12 (P2 Large Detector, all 64 ch) as running examples.

The pipeline goal is to measure, for each VMM configuration (gain sg, peaking time snt), the mean hit rate per good channel separately during beam-on (spill) and beam-off (inter-spill) periods. The ratio of these two rates is the fundamental figure of merit for configuration selection.

Step 1 — Trigger Rate Time Series

Spill structure visualisation

The trigger VMM 0, channel 40 hit timestamps are binned into 1 ms windows to produce a rate vs. time plot that directly reveals the SPS spill structure. This is the first sanity check: a working DAQ shows a clear two-level pattern — high rate during spills, near-zero between them. The bin width of 1 ms is chosen to collect ~4 triggers per bin at 5 kHz beam intensity, giving enough statistics to distinguish beam-on from beam-off while still resolving within-spill bunch microstructure.

The trigger reference channel (VMM 0, ch 40) is verified from the hits-per-channel plot of the trigger VMM: ch 40 stands out clearly above all others, confirming it carries the coincidence signal cleanly.

Step 2 — Spill Mask Construction

Beam-on / beam-off classification

Each 1 ms bin is classified as spill-on or spill-off by applying a fixed threshold of 1 kHz on the trigger rate. Two refinements are applied:

  • Hole-filling (max_gap_s = 2 s): short below-threshold gaps within a spill are reclassified as on-spill. This corrects for SPS bunch microstructure dips at low beam intensity without bridging real inter-spill gaps (which are always ≥ 5 s in this dataset).
  • Minimum spill duration (min_spill_s = 1 s): on-regions shorter than 1 s after hole-filling are discarded — typically run-start artefacts or isolated cosmic hits.

The 1 kHz threshold sits comfortably between the ~4–80 kHz on-spill rate and the ~0 kHz off-spill rate and is robust across the full range of beam intensities in both datasets.

Step 3a — Total Hit Count per Channel

Dead / noisy channel identification (total-hit criterion)

Hit counts are aggregated over all diagnostic runs for each VMM channel. Connected channels are defined from the detector geometry in vmm_mapping.py. Two thresholds are applied relative to the median count among connected channels:

  • Dead: count < 10% × median — disconnected or broken pad.
  • Noisy: count > 5× median — extreme self-triggering (total-hit level only; a finer noise test is applied in Step 3b).

Step 3b — Per-Channel Spill-On / Spill-Off Rate

Noise-based channel masking using off-spill rate

For each diagnostic run, per-channel hit rates are computed separately during on-spill and off-spill periods using the mask from Step 2. The off-spill rate is the true noise discriminator:

  • A channel with elevated off-spill rate is genuinely self-triggering on electronics noise and must be masked.
  • A channel with elevated on-spill rate only is a beam-hit pad — real signal, must not be masked.

Noisy threshold: off-spill rate > 3× median off-spill rate among non-dead connected channels of the same VMM. The threshold is shown as a dashed orange line on the spill-off panel. Masking is applied per configuration (sg, snt): a channel noisy at high gain is not excluded from low-gain configurations where it is clean.

Steps 4–5 — Rate vs. Configuration and Summary

Config scan rates and all-VMM comparison

For each configuration (sg, snt), all hits from good channels are accumulated into 1 ms bins and the spill mask is applied. Two per-VMM rates are computed:

  • rate_on_khz: mean on-spill hit rate ÷ n_good_channels — signal + noise, dominated by beam.
  • rate_off_khz: mean off-spill hit rate ÷ n_good_channels — pure electronics noise floor.

Normalisation by n_good_channels makes VMMs with different numbers of connected pads directly comparable. The ±1σ band across good channels quantifies beam illumination non-uniformity.

Key Observations

5 kHz vs 15 kHz comparison

  • Beam scaling: The nominal beam rate ratio is ~3×, yet the VMM on-spill rates scale by factors of 1.1–20× depending on the VMM and configuration. The small detector (VMM 11) is close to saturation at 5 kHz; the large detector (VMM 12) scales nearly linearly.
  • Noise floor: The off-spill rate at 15 kHz is 5–10× higher than at 5 kHz for the large detector at high gain (sg ≥ 4.5), consistent with cross-talk or charge-sharing at higher hit rates. The small detector noise floor is largely unaffected.
  • Best on/off ratio: VMM 11 achieves on/off > 800 at 15 kHz (sg=3.0, snt=200), versus ~600 at 5 kHz. VMM 12 reaches on/off ~565 at 15 kHz (sg=3.0, snt=200) vs ~62 at 5 kHz — a dramatic improvement driven by the much higher on-spill signal at 15 kHz.

VMM 11 vs VMM 12

  • VMM 11 (small detector, 50 ch active) consistently shows on-spill rates 30–60× higher than VMM 12 (large detector, 45–63 ch active) per channel, reflecting the difference in pad area: the small detector pads intercept a much larger fraction of the beam footprint.
  • VMM 12 good-channel count varies more across configurations (47–63) due to a small number of channels near the dead/noisy boundary at certain gain settings; VMM 11 maintains 50 good channels consistently.
  • For configuration optimisation the two VMMs point to the same conclusion: longer peaking time (snt=200 ns) at sg=4.5 or 6.0 maximises the signal rate, with the caveat that the noise floor must be monitored at sg=6.0 in the 15 kHz regime.
